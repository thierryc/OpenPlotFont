# Converting outline fonts to pen strokes: a research study

Research date: **1 October 2026**. Scope: ordinary outline fonts, especially Latin handwriting and script families available through Google Fonts; candidate centerlines, stroke reconstruction, width information, and a possible future OpenPlotFont authoring workflow.

This is a literature and design study. No converter was implemented, no font was converted, and no hardware was operated. Descriptions of published systems are distinguished from recommendations and proposed format work. Tool availability was checked through official documentation, author project pages, and public repositories; runtime compatibility and conversion quality were not tested.

## Main findings

**Useful conversion is possible, but there is no uniquely correct general inverse from a filled glyph to the pen movements that produced it.** A production pipeline should generate candidates, retain geometric evidence, expose ambiguity, and allow a designer to approve or revise the result.

For this project, my recommendations are:

| Objective | First route to investigate | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Highest fidelity when editable source exists | Recover authored skeleton paths and brush data from the source | Avoids solving an inverse problem unnecessarily |
| Font-specific automatic stroke decomposition | **StrokeStyles**, Berio et al., 2022 | Directly addresses recovering strokes from font shapes |
| Clean geometric skeletons from Bézier outlines | **FloMat** medial-axis and scale-axis transforms | Works with curved vector boundaries and retains radius evidence |
| A tractable Python baseline | FontTools plus controlled rasterization, scikit-image/SciPy, graph processing, and constrained curve fitting | Separates measurable geometry from inferred stroke decisions |
| Plausible traversal through script crossings | **Single-Line Drawing Vectorization**, Magne and Sorkine-Hornung, 2025 | Explicitly considers curve parameterization and crossing traversal |
| Most recent relevant line-art research | **Disney/ETH Bézier spline vectorization**, July 2026 | Combines semantic stroke grouping with differentiable fitting |
| Fast visual experiment | Inkscape centerline tracing / AutoTrace | Available tools for discovering whether a design is amenable to tracing |

These are judgments about relevance and evaluation priority, not results of a comparative benchmark. The font-specific recommendation is grounded in [StrokeStyles](https://www.doc.gold.ac.uk/autograff/post/papers/strokestyles_2022/); the geometric option is [FloMat](https://github.com/FlorisSteenkamp/MAT); the traversal option is [Single-Line Drawing Vectorization](https://igl.ethz.ch/projects/sld-vectorization/); the newest line-art option is [Disney Research's 2026 publication](https://studios.disneyresearch.com/2026/07/16/2d-gaussian-splatting-for-bezier-spline-line-art-vectorization/).

**Store inferred width before calling it pressure.** Distance from a centerline to the source boundary is geometric information. Pressure is a property of a writing process and a tool. A future font format can preserve width or an explicitly inferred expression profile, while a consuming application determines any physical force or Z movement.

The most elegant design for OpenPlotFont is a **reviewed stroke representation with optional geometric profiles and provenance**, generated by a hybrid geometric and semantic process. Its value is in retaining both a usable trajectory and an honest account of what was inferred.

## 1. Clarify what is being converted

### Closed paths are not inherently the problem

A real stroke font can contain a closed centerline: an `O` may be a loop that the pen traces once. A filled-outline `O`, however, normally has an outer boundary and an inner boundary. Plotting both boundaries produces two loops; converting it requires finding a trajectory between them.

The distinction is **drawing intent**:

| Input | Meaning | Required action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Open authored skeleton | A pen trajectory already exists | Preserve it |
| Closed authored skeleton | A pen trajectory intentionally loops | Preserve and trace once |
| Filled outline contours | Boundaries describe an ink region | Infer a new stroke representation, or retain the region as a fill |
| Artificially retraced or degenerate outline | A font workaround may encode a doubled path | Inspect its construction before attempting deduplication |
| Expanded authoring stroke | A skeleton may survive in the editable source | Recover source data when available |

This distinction already exists in [OpenPlotFont's format](OPENPLOTFONT_FORMAT.md). Opening a contour by removing its closing segment does not turn the contour into a centerline. It usually leaves almost the entire outer boundary intact.

### Monoline, single-line, single-stroke, and script are different claims

* **Monoline** describes roughly constant visual thickness. Its distributed TTF can still contain filled outlines.
* **Centerline or single-line font** describes the representation of each visible stroke.
* **Single-stroke glyph** describes the number of pen-down trajectories. Many centerline glyphs require several.
* **Script font** describes a visual or linguistic construction; it does not guarantee recoverable pen order or continuous drawing.

Conventional TrueType glyph geometry records contours and quadratic curves. That outline representation does not specify the historical writing motion. [Microsoft's TrueType fundamentals](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/typography/opentype/otspec170/ttch01)

A Google Fonts family therefore should not be classified as plot-ready merely because its specimen looks handwritten.

## 2. Why nobody can recover the original gesture in every case

### A forward model

Imagine a pen following a curve \(C(t)\), with a footprint \(K(t)\). Its resulting ink region is approximately

\[
\Omega = \bigcup_{t\in[0,1]} \left(C(t)+K(t)\right).
\]

Several strokes produce the union of several such swept regions. With a circular pen of radius \(r(t)\), each footprint is a disk. With a broad nib or brush, it can be anisotropic, rotated, asymmetric, or dependent on previous motion.

An outline font supplies a version of \(\partial\Omega\), the boundary of the resulting region. We want curves, footprints, directions, and a schedule. The input has discarded much of that information.

### Concrete ambiguities

**Direction:** drawing a constant-width stroke from left to right or right to left can produce the same outline.

**Order:** two crossing strokes generally have the same filled union regardless of which was drawn first. A solid binary silhouette provides no overlap darkness or temporal evidence.

**Retracing:** a script stem might be traversed down and up along the same route. The filled union cannot count how many times the pen passed over it.

**Crossing versus branching:** an `X`-shaped region can suggest two crossing strokes, a single self-intersecting trajectory, or multiple connected segments. Geometry constrains the options but does not always select one.

**Footprint versus trajectory:** a wider shape may arise from more force, a larger nib, a rotated broad nib, an offset path, several passes, or deliberate outline editing.

**Typography versus handwriting:** designers adjust joins, terminals, counters, and optical weight for legibility. Their outlines need not be exactly reproducible by any simple moving pen.

These examples establish non-uniqueness without needing an empirical failure rate. Different hidden processes can yield the same visible set.

### What can still be recovered

We can recover a useful *candidate* that satisfies chosen objectives: follows the design, has plausible continuity, separates marks, retains loops, requires a reasonable number of lifts, and reproduces the source under a declared brush model.

A designer-approved candidate becomes an authored derivative. It should not be described as the original recovered pen motion unless the source actually retained that motion.

The difficult task also appears in offline-to-online handwriting research. **TRACE** uses a learned model to infer temporal trajectories from handwriting images, while **FINet**, published in January 2025, addresses Chinese handwriting trajectory reconstruction. Both are evidence that sequence recovery is a distinct research problem, with domain-specific data and priors. Neither establishes reliable conversion of arbitrary Latin script fonts. [TRACE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.11559), [FINet](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0031320324007003)

## 3. The mathematical foundation: medial axes and radius functions

### The medial-axis transform

For an interior point \(x\), define its boundary distance

\[
d(x)=\inf_{y\in\partial\Omega}\|x-y\|.
\]

The medial axis consists of centers of maximal disks contained in the region. “Maximal” means a disk is not contained in a larger inscribed disk; it does not mean only the largest disk in the glyph. The medial-axis transform pairs those centers with their radii.

Under the appropriate domain assumptions, the full continuous transform reconstructs the shape from the union of its medial disks. Its graph preserves topological information, including holes. This is why a skeleton plus radius is a compelling shape representation. It does **not** prove that the graph is the writer's trajectory. [Choi, Choi and Moon, mathematical theory of the medial-axis transform, 1997](https://msp.org/pjm/1997/181-1/pjm-v181-n1-p02-s.pdf)

### Radius is useful, but twice the radius is not universally stroke width

At a centerline location \(C(s)\), the medial radius is

\[
r(s)=d(C(s)).
\]

In a locally symmetric constant-width ribbon, \(2r\) is a good thickness estimate. At a crossing, terminal, acute bend, or asymmetric shape, it may measure something different from the intended stroke's width.

An illustrative counterexample is the intersection of two perpendicular strips, each of full width \(w\). At their center, the nearest boundary of the union is \(w/\sqrt{2}\) away. Thus \(2d=\sqrt{2}w\), larger than either constituent stroke's width. Turning that value directly into a pressure command would introduce a false pressure peak at the crossing.

For a selected stroke, a second estimate is its **normal cross-section**. Let \(n(s)\) be a unit normal to the trajectory. Intersect the line \(C(s)+u n(s)\) with the relevant boundaries and estimate

\[
w_{\perp}(s)=u_+(s)-u_-(s).
\]

Also retain the two side distances separately. Their asymmetry can reveal that a geometric skeleton differs from the desired pen spine. At overlaps, the relevant boundary may be hidden; width then needs interpolation, decomposition, or explicit uncertainty.

These are different measurements. A future profile must identify which one it stores.

### A variable-width stroke is an envelope, not two simple offsets

There is a useful further distinction between a writer's spine and a shape's medial axis. For a disk sweep along an arc-length-parameterized curve, let \(T\) and \(N\) be its unit tangent and normal, and let \(r'=dr/ds\). A candidate boundary envelope follows from solving both

\[
\|x-C(s)\|^2-r(s)^2=0
\quad\text{and}\quad
\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\bigl(\|x-C(s)\|^2-r(s)^2\bigr)=0.
\]

Differentiating gives \((x-C)\cdot T=-rr'\). For a regular envelope with \(|r'|<1\), this yields

\[
x_\pm(s)=C(s)+r(s)\left[-r'(s)T(s)
\pm\sqrt{1-r'(s)^2}\,N(s)\right].
\]

Only when radius is locally constant does this reduce to the familiar normal offsets \(C\pm rN\). The contact points of a changing-radius footprint are shifted along the tangent. Other disks can hide parts of this envelope, and some disks in an authored sweep can be redundant rather than maximal.

This derivation explains why extracting normal midpoints or a medial axis need not return the original spine of a tapered brush stroke, even with a perfectly clean outline. The inverse needs a declared sweep model, and the resulting candidate must be assessed against that model.

### Why raw medial axes have unwanted branches

Small bumps on an outline can create long skeleton branches. Square or pointed terminals generate branches that describe their shape, rather than a simple writing spine. Serifs and ink traps add genuine geometry that may or may not belong in a plotter adaptation.

Consequently, “compute the medial axis” is only the beginning. Simplification must retain counters and important terminals while suppressing artifacts according to the intended adaptation.

### Scale-aware pruning

The **scale-axis transform** introduces a scale-dependent skeletal representation based on scaling medial balls. It provides a principled way to simplify shapes according to local size, rather than deleting every branch shorter than an arbitrary number of pixels. Simplification still changes represented detail and does not recover stroke order. [Giesen, Miklos, Pauly and Wormser, 2009](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/bitstreams/dc44b74e-138a-4bba-9fc0-bf2e5b45a7cd/download)

**Variational Pruning of Medial Axes of Planar Shapes**, Rong and Ju, 2023, proposes a significance measure derived from retaining a connected subset that favors alignment with the boundary. The authors report less unwanted shortening and better preservation of features across scales; their project provides code. This is worth evaluating for delicate script terminals. [Author project](https://peter-rong.github.io/personal_homepage/publication_page/variational_pruning.html)

**Skeleton Sparsification and Densification Scale-Spaces**, Gierke and Peter, published 8 July 2026, develops hierarchical simplification and growth of skeletal representations. Its experiments include skeleton robustness, compression, and manufacturing-related shape processing. It informs multi-scale candidate selection; it is not a demonstrated script-font converter. [Journal paper](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10851-026-01319-4)

For stronger mathematical guarantees, Lieutier and Wintraecken's **\((\lambda,\alpha)\)-medial axis** supplies quantified stability results under stated assumptions. That work explains why stable subsets matter when computing from approximate boundaries. A stability theorem is not a certificate of typographic correctness. [STOC 2023 full paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.04014)

My proposed pruning policy would combine dimensionless branch significance, reconstruction loss, persistence across scales, and designer-marked terminals. No single length threshold should decide whether a flourish is expendable.

## 4. Compare the algorithm families

| Family | What it produces | Strength | Main limitation for script fonts |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Binary thinning | A thin pixel skeleton | Simple baseline; useful topology | Pixel bias; no guaranteed geometric centering or writing schedule |
| Distance-based medial axis | Skeleton with radius evidence | Natural geometric thickness information | Spurious branches and overlap inflation |
| Vector medial axis / boundary Voronoi methods | Geometric skeleton graph | Exploits precise font outlines | Curve approximation and robust topology require care |
| Straight skeleton | Inward polygon-wavefront graph | Useful for polygon offsetting | Not the Euclidean medial axis in general |
| Paired-boundary midpoint fitting | A spine between matched boundary pieces | Excellent where correspondence is known | Finding correspondence is the hard part |
| Font-specific perceptual decomposition | Candidate constituent strokes | Handles shape structure beyond thinning | Parameters, ambiguous cases, research implementation complexity |
| Frame / PolyVector fields | Directional continuation through junctions | Models multiple crossing directions | A directional field is not a complete stroke schedule |
| Learned trajectory prediction | Ordered curves or point sequences | Can supply missing semantic priors | Training-domain bias and hallucinated geometry |
| Inverse graphics with a brush model | Curves and appearance fitted to source | Jointly refines geometry and profile | Non-unique solutions; topology needs initialization or search |

### A. Raster thinning and distance transforms

scikit-image distinguishes `skeletonize`, which performs thinning, from `medial_axis`, which uses the distance transform and can return distance values. These are useful building blocks, rather than full font-conversion tools. SciPy's Euclidean distance transform supports explicit grid spacing, which matters when translating raster measurements back to font units. [scikit-image morphology documentation](https://scikit-image.org/docs/stable/api/skimage.morphology), [SciPy distance-transform documentation](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.ndimage.distance_transform_edt.html)

My proposed baseline would render unhinted glyphs on a padded grid, record the exact transform, skeletonize at multiple resolutions, collapse junction blobs, classify branches, then fit curves under geometric constraints. Rendering resolution should be chosen from the thinnest meaningful feature and a measurable error target, not a fixed “high DPI” label.

An isolated diagonal and a narrow hairline must survive rasterization. Threshold changes must not silently close a counter or fuse two strokes. Convergence between resolutions is evidence of numerical stability, though it cannot settle writing ambiguity.

### B. Curved vector medial axes

**FloMat** is a JavaScript/TypeScript library for medial and scale-axis transforms of shapes bounded by linear, quadratic, and cubic Bézier curves, including holes and multiple loops. It returns graph structures and curve representations. Its README explains that holes are cut in its internal tree representation, so a downstream adapter must preserve their actual cyclic geometry. The project describes a topology-preserving SAT variant; that implementation should not be conflated with every theoretical SAT definition. [FloMat repository and documentation](https://github.com/FlorisSteenkamp/MAT)

This is an attractive geometric engine to evaluate, even for a Python-led workflow. It avoids making rasterization the sole geometric authority. However, a medial curve of a Bézier-bounded region is generally not itself an exact low-degree Bézier. Returned curves still require measured approximation quality.

A polygonal Voronoi approach is another option, provided the boundary is sampled adaptively and robust predicates handle degeneracies. A Voronoi diagram of a few boundary vertices is not automatically a faithful medial axis of the entire curved glyph.

### C. Straight skeletons

CGAL explains that a straight skeleton measures equidistance to supporting lines of polygon edges; for non-convex shapes it can differ from the Euclidean medial axis. It is designed around polygon offsetting and related geometry. [CGAL manual](https://doc.cgal.org/6.1/Straight_skeleton_2/index.html)

I would not choose it as the default for script centerlines with width estimates. A method useful for mitered offsets or roofs need not identify a plausible round-pen spine. It remains a legitimate comparison method for particular polygonal designs.

### D. Boundary pairing and midpoint curves

If two corresponding boundary curves \(A(t)\) and \(B(t)\) have already been identified, their midpoint is

\[
C(t)=\tfrac12(A(t)+B(t)).
\]

For equal-degree Béziers using compatible parameterizations, averaging matching control points represents that midpoint exactly. But equal parameter values do not necessarily identify opposite points across the stroke. Different node placement, unequal arc lengths, caps, intersections, and broad-nib asymmetry break naive pairing.

For assisted editing, a designer can identify opposite boundary pieces and endpoints. A more sophisticated solver can seek matching normals or monotone boundary correspondence. These methods are especially appealing for near-monoline stems, and much less decisive where several strokes merge.

### E. Stroke decomposition and directional fields

At a junction, neighboring skeleton edges have possible continuations. A straight continuation may indicate a crossing; a smooth turn may indicate a loop; an endpoint may terminate against another stroke. Local tangents alone are insufficient in many ornate scripts.

**PolyVector vectorization**, Bessmeltsev and Solomon, models multiple directions through junctions to improve connectivity. It is an important conceptual bridge from “a skeleton pixel has neighbors” to “several strokes pass through this location.” Its line-drawing setting should be distinguished from arbitrary font outline reconstruction. [Research paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.01922)

The proposed conversion process should keep alternative continuations when evidence is weak. Graph connectivity, selected stroke decomposition, and final stroke order are three separate representations.

## 5. Font-specific and newer research: what has actually been achieved

### StrokeStyles: the strongest direct match to this task

Berio, Leymarie, Asente and Echevarria's **StrokeStyles: Stroke-Based Segmentation and Stylization of Fonts** (2022) decomposes glyphs into overlapping or intersecting strokes. It combines medial-axis analysis, convex and concave shape features, links between concavities, junction classification, and perceptual continuation. It needs no training set or glyph templates and addresses multiple writing systems and styles. [Paper and author project](https://www.doc.gold.ac.uk/autograff/post/papers/strokestyles_2022/)

This is substantially closer to the user's request than generic skeletonization. Its published aim includes stylization; suitability for a curated Google Fonts plotter library still needs evaluation.

The public implementation has Python segmentation code plus supporting Python modules and C++ geometry bindings. Its installation document describes CMake, SWIG, and numerous dependencies, with historical environment instructions. It should be treated as research software whose current buildability remains unverified here. [Repository](https://github.com/autograff-devel/strokestyles), [installation documentation](https://github.com/autograff-devel/strokestyles/blob/master/INSTALL.md)

Its output documentation is especially relevant: each glyph can contain stroke connectivity, the original sampled shape, spine polylines, per-segment `W` and per-vertex `Wv` width profiles, and stroke areas. The documented coordinate convention is top-left-origin, so integration must recover the original font transform and convert to OpenPlotFont's Y-up convention. The exact width convention must be checked before interpreting values as full width or radius. [Segmentation format](https://github.com/autograff-devel/strokestyles/blob/master/SEGMENTATION_FORMAT.md)

**Assessment:** evaluate first for font decomposition and width-profile recovery. Expect a dedicated adapter, typographic review, and build qualification; it is not an available OpenPlotFont importer.

### Single-Line Drawing Vectorization: a stronger model of traversal

Magne and Sorkine-Hornung's 2025 method fits a parameterized Bézier spline to a drawing with self-intersections. It uses a graph, geometric priors, and a trained intersection classifier; the authors also describe extension to multiple strokes and interactive correction. Its focus is plausible traversal, which matters directly to script loops and crossings. Its benchmark concerns single-line drawings, not a broad font collection. [ETH project](https://igl.ethz.ch/projects/sld-vectorization/)

The official repository provides Python code with compiled C++ components, model-weight download instructions, SVG output, batch processing, and a GUI. It documents Python 3.9–3.12 and Potrace-related dependencies. I did not identify an explicit top-level license in the inspected repository listing; verify reuse terms, model terms, and benchmark-image rights before integration. Public code alone does not establish redistribution permission. [Official implementation](https://github.com/tanguymagne/SLD-Vectorization)

**Assessment:** a high-priority candidate for near-monoline script bodies. Do not force every glyph into its single-curve assumption: dots, accents, crossbars, and genuinely separate strokes remain separate.

### Disney/ETH, July 2026: semantic grouping and differentiable fitting

**2D Gaussian Splatting for Bézier Spline Line Art Vectorization** uses predicted depth and semantic features to divide a skeleton graph into candidate stroke groups. It then fits Bézier geometry and appearance through differentiable Gaussian rendering, with user correction. The paper reports state-of-the-art image reconstruction for its evaluated line-art task. [Official publication page](https://studios.disneyresearch.com/2026/07/16/2d-gaussian-splatting-for-bezier-spline-line-art-vectorization/)

**Assessment:** among the most advanced relevant systems found in this review. Its semantic priors and appearance model may transfer imperfectly to glyphs. Reconstruction quality, brush texture, and plausible artistic strokes are different objectives from a compact font with reliable pen-up boundaries. The inspected publication page offers papers; it does not establish a ready-to-install converter or verified code license.

### LLM-generated font skeletons, ACCV 2024

Liu et al. fine-tuned a language model to predict SVG-like skeleton commands from outlines using **8,213 Japanese outline/skeleton pairs**. Their study reports improvement over a PolyVector baseline on its metrics, but identifies limitations across styles and weaker results for simpler categories including Latin letters. [ACCV paper](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/ACCV2024/html/Liu_Fine-tuning_Large_Language_Models_for_Automatic_Font_Skeleton_Generation_Exploration_ACCV_2024_paper.html)

**Assessment:** evidence that semantic inference can help, not evidence that a general LLM can faithfully convert Google Script fonts. Treat model output as a proposal to verify against geometry, glyph identity, and stroke constraints.

### Faster inverse graphics is advancing, but is a separate component

**DiffVG** supplies differentiable vector rendering for editing and learning. It enables optimization of vector parameters against images; it does not by itself recover a correct stroke topology. [Original paper](https://people.csail.mit.edu/tzumao/diffvg/diffvg.pdf)

**Bézier Splatting**, presented at NeurIPS 2025 according to its current author record, uses Gaussians sampled along curves and adaptive primitive changes to accelerate differentiable fitting. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.16424)

**CubicSplat**, posted August 2026, develops error-bounded forward relaxation for vector rasterization; its reported benchmarks include closed-fill image reconstruction. It is relevant to optimization infrastructure, not proof of font stroke recovery. [Author preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20803)

My preferred application of such technology is **refinement after topology selection**: fit a small set of candidate curves and a declared footprint model. An unconstrained image optimizer could reproduce a glyph with many tiny strokes while completely failing the intended plotter representation.

### Handwriting synthesis and skeleton-guided font generation

The 2025 **Layout Stroke Imitation** preprint predicts handwriting strokes conditioned on style and word layout. It illustrates a direction for generating temporal trajectories, rather than just raster text. It does not establish exact recovery of a chosen font's outlines. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.15678)

Likewise, a paper that uses skeletons to generate filled fonts solves the opposite direction from the requested conversion. **Skeleton and Font Generation Network** (2025) is one such example. It is relevant background for structural representations, rather than a drop-in outline-to-stroke solution. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.08062)

## 6. What Glyphs and existing applications can contribute

### Glyphs is an appropriate review environment

The current official Glyphs Handbook documents strokes on open or closed paths, editable widths and heights, caps, joins, and **pen points** that modulate width, height, angle, and placement. These are authoring capabilities for drawing and expanding strokes. They do not constitute a documented automatic inverse from arbitrary filled outlines. [Glyphs Handbook: Strokes](https://handbook.glyphsapp.com/strokes/)

The older monoline tutorial explicitly refers to Glyphs 2 and earlier. Its useful conceptual distinction is to author a skeleton and expand it for ordinary font export. Its historic UI instructions should not be assumed valid for Glyphs 4. [Official monoline tutorial](https://glyphsapp.com/learn/monoline)

The mekkablue script collection lists **Center Line**, which creates centerlines between selected segments and their opposites, with an option to place them in the background. That is promising for local assisted editing. Its description does not promise whole-font segmentation, ordering, or Glyphs 4 compatibility. [Author's script collection](https://github.com/mekkablue/Glyphs-Scripts)

**LTTR/INK** is another relevant authoring model: a skeleton plus adjustable brush dimensions and angle creates outlines. If a font's original source used this approach and preserves that data, recovering it could be much better than skeletonizing its compiled outline. The vendor documentation establishes the forward workflow, not a general reverse converter. [Drawing documentation](https://www.lttrink.com/documentation/start-drawing-lttrink-glyphs/)

The proposed Glyphs workflow is to keep the untouched outline as a reference layer, place candidate strokes in a separate working copy, and review crossings, termini, loops, direction, and ordering. Only reviewed source geometry should pass into the existing selected-master OpenPlotFont export route.

The current [OpenPlotFont Glyphs workflow](GLYPHS_AND_EXPORT.md) documents geometry export and its compatibility limits. It does not define pen-point/profile serialization. Native Glyphs support for a property does not automatically imply that the OpenPlotFont exporter preserves it.

### Inkscape and AutoTrace

Inkscape's official tracing tutorial describes **Centerline tracing (autotrace)** for obtaining editable strokes from line drawings. AutoTrace's own README documents outline and centerline modes. These make a useful visual experiment: render a glyph as a bitmap, trace its centerline, and inspect the result. [Inkscape tutorial](https://inkscape.org/en/doc/tutorials/tracing/tutorial-tracing.ru.html/), [AutoTrace](https://github.com/autotrace/autotrace/blob/master/README.md)

That experiment gives drawing geometry. Reconstructing a reusable font still requires mappings, metrics, alternate glyphs, shaping, source attribution, and reviewed stroke scheduling. Generic tracing will not supply these.

### FontForge

FontForge documents an autotracing workflow through external AutoTrace or Potrace, rather than a native autotracer. It accepts bitmap backgrounds and lets users pass tracing arguments. This is a potential batch authoring aid, not documented evidence of a turnkey script-font conversion system. [Official documentation](https://fontforge.org/docs/techref/autotrace.html)

Potrace traces outlines. Its appearance in a sophisticated centerline pipeline can be an intermediate boundary-reconstruction step; it should not itself be called a centerline extractor.

### LingDong's projects

**skeleton-tracing** converts a binary skeleton representation into polylines and includes Python among its implementations. It is useful for raster-to-graph work, but its outputs still need topology, trajectory, and curve review. [Repository](https://github.com/LingDong-/skeleton-tracing)

**chinese-hershey-font** demonstrates large-scale outline/raster-derived single-line font generation using multi-angle scans and segment merging. Its README identifies Python 2 dependencies and polyline JSON output. This is evidence that automated font batches are feasible; it does not establish Latin cursive quality, faithful width recovery, or inherited font-license clearance. [Repository](https://github.com/LingDong-/chinese-hershey-font)

The [existing stroke-font candidate study](STROKE_FONT_CANDIDATES.md) already distinguishes these inferred skeletons from authored stroke sources. A reviewed conversion project is a separate workflow from importing known centerlines.

## 7. From a skeleton graph to independent pen trajectories

This is the most important design step after geometric extraction.

Suppose a skeleton is a graph \(G=(V,E)\). A stroke is an ordered walk along selected geometric edges, possibly with repeated edges. Different walks can have the same geometric union.

At each ambiguous junction, a conversion system must decide:

1. Which incoming branch continues into which outgoing branch?
2. Which branch terminates there?
3. Is a crossing real, or an artifact of the skeleton?
4. Is retracing plausible or necessary?
5. Where does a pen lift occur?
6. Which endpoint is the start, and which direction is preferred?

A proposed continuation score could combine tangent agreement, curvature, width consistency, boundary-pair evidence, nearby letter structure, and author hints. This is a design proposal, not an implemented algorithm or a claim that the cited systems use this exact combination.

Prefer family-wide consistency: similar stems, bowls, and terminals should not receive unrelated interpretations merely because each glyph was optimized independently. But do not force one rule onto dissimilar uppercase and lowercase constructions.

### Why minimizing pen lifts is insufficient

For an undirected connected graph, an Euler traversal uses every edge once only when there are zero or two odd-degree vertices. A more highly branching skeleton needs separate trails or repeated edges if every edge is retained.

The Chinese-postman objective can reduce lifts by retracing. In a pen drawing, retracing may darken ink and alter appearance; it can also produce implausible handwriting. Thus geometric coverage and convenient machine travel do not establish a desirable authored schedule.

An `i` still has a separate dot. A `t` often has a separate crossbar. A flourish crossing a stem does not automatically mean the pen changes from one to the other there.

### Ordered strokes must be retained as a design decision

Once reviewed, the output should preserve each stroke's boundary, start, direction, and order. A consumer's optional travel optimization should not silently erase these decisions.

The adaptation may intentionally choose a different order from historical handwriting. Document it as the derivative's drawing schedule. There is no need to claim a recovered history to obtain a useful plotter font.

## 8. Width, pressure, nib angle, and what the outline can tell us

### The most useful first channel is geometric thickness

Preserving the source's thickness variations can support future variable-width previews, calligraphic reconstruction, adaptation comparisons, and eventually tool-specific rendering. Even a fixed-width plotter can use the centerline now while the author retains profile evidence for later.

Use explicit names and units: medial radius in font units, normal-section full width in font units, or a dimensionless expression value. Avoid one ambiguous field called `pressure` for all three.

### Width changes can be caused by nib orientation alone

For an ideal fixed elliptical footprint with principal semiaxes \(a,b\) and orientation vectors \(u,v\), its extent in the curve-normal direction \(n\) is

\[
w(n)=2\sqrt{a^2(n\cdot u)^2+b^2(n\cdot v)^2}.
\]

As the trajectory turns, \(n\) changes. Apparent thickness can therefore change while force and footprint dimensions stay constant. This expression describes a local idealized support width; caps, curvature, overlap, and brush dynamics complicate actual swept shapes.

A broad-nib script should consequently not be interpreted as a round pen whose pressure rises whenever the stroke becomes wide. A single scalar also cannot reproduce a footprint's orientation and asymmetry.

### A realistic physical mapping needs calibration

A tool's width could be modeled schematically as

\[
w_{\mathrm{mm}} = f(F,\theta,\phi,v,\text{ink},\text{paper},\text{history}).
\]

Here \(F\) is tip force; the angles describe orientation; \(v\) is drawing speed. A future consumer might invert a calibrated, restricted version of this function when possible. A fixed-width pen or a saturating brush may not offer a usable inverse for all desired widths.

This is why neither `pressure = width` nor `Z = width` is a portable interpretation. Pressure in the physical sense also differs from force: pressure involves contact area. In digital-pen contexts, the word often denotes a device signal proportional to force.

We should also avoid assuming that no existing robot or plotting arrangement can vary force. The useful requirement is device-independent storage that does not depend on whether a particular consumer can realize it.

### There is an established interchange precedent

W3C **InkML** explicitly distinguishes pen tip force (`F`), stroke width (`W`), brush dimensions, orientation, time, and contact state. Its specification supports units and normalization. It is an excellent precedent for separating measured dynamics from visual width, though it is an ink interchange format rather than a substitute for OpenPlotFont's glyph and layout model. [InkML Recommendation](https://www.w3.org/TR/InkML/)

### What should be stored when only an outline is available?

| Quantity | What can be claimed | Proposed treatment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Boundary-distance radius | Computed geometric evidence | Retain with method and error information |
| Stroke-specific width | Estimated after selecting a trajectory/decomposition | Retain with uncertainty near overlaps |
| Left/right side distances | Geometric asymmetry relative to a chosen spine | Useful optional analysis data |
| Brush size and angle | Fitted latent parameters under a declared model | Label inferred and model-dependent |
| Relative expression | A designed or inferred visual control | Define normalization; do not claim physical force |
| Original tip force | Absent from ordinary outline geometry | Unknown unless actual source dynamics exist |
| Actual machine force or Z | Determined for a physical setup | Keep in consumer/tool settings |

At a crossing, keep unknown values unknown. Do not encode unknown force as zero: zero can mean no contact. A confidence score needs a definition; an arbitrary heuristic score should not masquerade as a calibrated probability.

## 9. A proposed architecture for converting many Google Fonts

The following is a workflow proposal. None of these conversion stages has been added to OpenPlotFont.

### Stage 1: inventory the real source

For each family, pin the distributed font file, upstream revision or digest, style, variation coordinates, and exact license. Look for `.glyphs`, UFO, design-source brush data, or existing stroke adaptations before inference.

An open-source outline font does not necessarily come with the original pen skeleton. Source may be present but contain the same outline geometry as the compiled font.

Keep an inventory of every glyph, including unencoded alternates and ligatures. A Unicode-only list is insufficient for a script with contextual substitutions.

### Stage 2: resolve one static design

For a variable family, choose a specific axis location. Resolve component transforms while keeping the original source separate. Extract curves, bearings, advances, and glyph identity consistently.

FontTools documents access to glyph sets at chosen variation locations. It is a suitable extraction component, not a stroke inference algorithm. [FontTools TTFont documentation](https://fonttools.readthedocs.io/en/stable/ttLib/ttFont.html)

Do not assume that the lightest weight has the best skeleton. It may preserve narrow separation at a join, but it may also reduce hairlines below a robust sampling scale. Compare selected weights rather than silently extrapolating to zero weight.

### Stage 3: prepare regions without losing evidence

Interpret fill rules and compound contours correctly. Preserve holes. If overlapped components need a geometric union for the solver, do it on an analysis copy and retain the original pieces: those pieces may contain valuable stroke clues.

Avoid aggressive simplification, automatic contour closure, or optical adjustments before analysis. A broken region should produce a diagnostic, not invented geometry.

### Stage 4: generate more than one candidate

Compare a raster baseline, a vector medial-axis candidate, and—where practical—a font-specific decomposition. Save the raw candidate separately from pruned and fitted versions.

Agreement between methods is useful evidence. Disagreement often identifies the glyphs that most need review. It should trigger investigation rather than selecting the smoothest-looking preview automatically.

### Stage 5: decide topology, then fit curves

Choose junction continuation and stroke grouping before optimizing Bézier control points. Retain authored or reviewed endpoint constraints, corners, counters, and loops.

A proposed fitting objective is

\[
E = \alpha E_{\mathrm{shape}} + \beta E_{\mathrm{center}}
  + \gamma E_{\mathrm{smooth}} + \delta E_{\mathrm{complexity}}
  + \eta E_{\mathrm{profile}}.
\]

Shape agreement measures a declared reconstruction, center agreement measures placement relative to the relevant stroke boundaries, smoothness discourages accidental oscillation, complexity favors editable curves, and profile regularization suppresses unexplained width spikes.

Weights do not make this objective universal. Topology and endpoint conditions should be enforced explicitly. Extremely good image overlap should not be allowed to buy an unwanted bridge between strokes.

### Stage 6: retain profiles and unresolved cases

Measure radius on the raw geometric skeleton where possible. Estimate trajectory-specific width after decomposition. When smoothing moves the spine, remeasure or refit its associated profile rather than copying old samples blindly.

Flag uncertain crossings, very narrow gaps, small disconnected components, endpoint choices, and large disagreements between reconstruction and source. Glyph-level confidence alone is too coarse for many script problems.

### Stage 7: review in Glyphs

Use separate source copies and reference layers. Review glyphs individually and in shaped words. Show centerlines with direction arrows and stroke numbers, along with the original region and a reconstructed-width preview.

For ambiguous glyphs, provide a small number of competing candidates rather than a single irreversible answer. Approval should cover trajectory boundaries, direction, order, closure, and profile interpretation.

### Stage 8: publish a reviewed derivative only after validation

Use the existing OpenPlotFont geometry export route for the reviewed strokes, with appropriate notices. Preserve curves in font units. A future pressure/profile extension would need its own defined export and compatibility work.

For unsupported or unattractive adaptations, retain explicit fill intent when appropriate or exclude the glyph/family with a diagnostic. Silently plotting filled boundaries is not an equivalent conversion.

## 10. Script layout is part of the conversion problem

Centerlines alone do not preserve the behavior of a font. A family may use contextual alternates, ligatures, language rules, cursive attachment, and mark positioning.

OpenType distinguishes glyph substitution through GSUB from positioning through GPOS. A converter must retain the glyphs referenced by those rules and preserve a coherent glyph mapping. [Microsoft OpenType layout overview](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/typography/opentype/spec/chapter2)

The current [OpenPlotFont layout profile](OPENPLOTFONT_LAYOUT.md) provides optional compiled static layout. Matching a converted repertoire to source layout requires validation; a visually close drawing is not enough if a ligature disappears or a mark is placed incorrectly.

Three separate decisions are required:

1. **Shaping:** which glyph variant is selected?
2. **Positioning:** where is that glyph placed?
3. **Drawing connection:** may the pen continue into the next glyph?

A GPOS cursive attachment or coincident outline shape does not authorize a pen-down join. Entry and exit endpoints need explicit trajectory semantics and a reviewed connector policy.

Dots and crossbars complicate word-level handwriting. Drawing a body, then its dot, then the next body is a different schedule from completing an entire word's bodies before adding marks. Neither schedule should be inferred from a contour array.

OpenPlotFont's current connection references require entry on the first operation and exit on the last, both open strokes. Its reference renderer rejects joining in OpenType mode pending a cluster-aware schedule. These are real compatibility constraints for a converted script, not problems solved by skeletonization. [Format connection rules](OPENPLOTFONT_FORMAT.md)

## 11. A possible future profile model for OpenPlotFont

This section proposes research directions. It does not change draft 0.3.

### Current contract

OpenPlotFont 0.3 stores trajectory geometry and mixed stroke/fill intent. It has no standardized per-point width or force channel. The specification places pen pressure and physical tool settings outside the font. Namespaced glyph `userData` can preserve inert analysis notes, but cannot redefine geometry or operate a machine. Unknown fields do not confer new rendering semantics. [Current specification](OPENPLOTFONT_FORMAT.md)

Consequently, there are two distinct possibilities:

* **Archival analysis now:** retain inferred measurements as clearly identified, non-operative research data, with no promise of standardized export, editing, or rendering.
* **Portable visual profiles later:** define versioned fields and validation so consumers understand precisely how a profile relates to a stroke and what unsupported profiles mean.

### Candidate profile quantities

| Conceptual quantity | Units | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Medial radius | Font units | Radius of an inscribed disk at a geometric medial location |
| Normal-section width | Font units | Full width measured across a selected trajectory |
| Left/right offsets | Font units | Asymmetric placement relative to a selected spine |
| Relative expression | Dimensionless | Intended variation under a documented normalization |
| Inferred brush footprint | Font units and angles | Parameters of a specified visual model |
| Confidence / validity | Defined score and/or flags | Reliability of a measurement or inference |
| Provenance | Descriptive metadata | Source, method, settings, review status |

Physical pen diameter, servo angles, motion speed, acceleration, and machine calibration remain consumer concerns. A geometric profile describes the source design; it should not prescribe which real nib is installed.

### Bind profiles to curves, not just to Bézier nodes

An on-curve node list is not an adequate general sampling model. Thickness can vary inside a segment, and Bézier parameter \(t\) is not proportional to traveled distance.

Two binding strategies deserve comparison:

* A stable segment identifier plus local parameter \(t\), with defined remapping after segment splitting or editing.
* Normalized arc length \(u=s/L\), with a defined rule for resampling after geometry changes.

Segment-local binding is precise but topology-sensitive. Arc-length binding is convenient for traversal but can shift semantic features after an edit. Neither remains correct through arbitrary edits automatically.

A future design needs interpolation rules, nonnegative geometric values, explicit missing samples, corner discontinuities, duplicate-position rules, and periodic behavior at closed-loop seams. Profiles should be functions along a trajectory, not literal physical time records unless real time data exists.

### Transform behavior matters

Under uniform scaling, widths and radii scale with coordinates, while dimensionless expression remains unchanged. Under reflection, left/right profiles and nib orientation require defined transformation behavior.

Under nonuniform scaling, a circle becomes an ellipse. Simply multiplying every scalar width by one factor can be wrong. A richer footprint description, a restricted transform contract, or remeasurement is required.

Reversing a stroke reverses its sample parameterization; it may also change asymmetric or directional interpretations. Rotating the start of a loop changes the profile seam. Travel optimization therefore has more semantic consequences once profiles exist.

### Explicit fallback behavior

A future profile could be optional visual information whose documented fallback is the same centerline with a consumer-selected pen. Alternatively, exact-width reproduction could be required, in which case an unsupported consumer must reject it.

Those are different capabilities. Decide the fallback contract before adding fields. Never depend on unknown-field tolerance to discard a required visual effect silently.

### Recommendation

First define **geometric source profiles and provenance**. Consider a dimensionless expression channel only with clear authored-versus-inferred labeling. Actual force measurements, if ever imported from digital ink, need distinct semantics and units. Consumers can later map desired appearance to calibrated force or another rendering strategy.

## 12. A Google Fonts evaluation corpus

The table below proposes a pilot, rather than recommending immediate bulk conversion. Difficulty classifications are hypotheses to test; no glyphs from these families were processed in this study.

| Pilot source | Why include it | What to test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Sacramento** | Its upstream description calls it monoline and semi-connected | Near-monoline baseline, loops, terminals, and joins |
| **Caveat**, at chosen weights | Upstream describes contextual variations | Thickness changes, alternates, feature coverage, and repeated letters |
| **Pinyon Script** | Ornate formal script; also connected to an existing stroke-adaptation ecosystem | Hairlines, contrast, flourishes, crossings, and path scheduling |
| An authored stroke font already in the library | Provides known trajectory data | Whether expansion and reconversion preserve plausible topology |
| A serif or deliberately non-calligraphic display face | Provides a negative control | Whether the pipeline recognizes that a clean pen spine may be inappropriate |

The source classifications and features are supported by [Sacramento's upstream description](https://github.com/google/fonts/blob/main/ofl/sacramento/DESCRIPTION.en_us.html), [Caveat's upstream description](https://github.com/google/fonts/blob/main/ofl/caveat/DESCRIPTION.en_us.html), and [Pinyon Script's font log](https://github.com/google/fonts/blob/main/ofl/pinyonscript/FONTLOG.txt).

The repository's [stroke candidate catalog](STROKE_FONT_CANDIDATES.md) already records a `Custom-Script` SVG adaptation with Pinyon ancestry. It is useful comparison material, subject to its documented attribution gaps. Its existence is not evidence that a generic automatic converter produced it. [Custom stroke-font authoring project](https://github.com/Shriinivas/inkscapestrokefont)

### Glyphs and strings to include

Use simple stems, bowls, loops, sharp corners, crossings, disconnected marks, and punctuation. Representative Latin candidates are `I O o e a n m u v w f g j i t x & @`, selected uppercase flourishes, numerals, accents, space, and the missing glyph.

Review repeated letters and joins in strings such as “minimum”, “office”, “little”, “jiffy”, and “affix”, plus the family’s supported ligatures, accented words, and punctuation. Test with the source font's actual shaping rules instead of presuming that every family contains the same features.

Include at least one isolated closed centerline loop, two independent strokes crossing without a trajectory join, a retraced path, and a compound region with a hole.

### Ground truth is scarce and sometimes plural

An existing authored stroke font can be expanded using a declared synthetic brush and then converted back. This supplies known curves for numerical testing. However, a clean synthetic round-pen dataset is easier than optically edited outline fonts and can bias evaluation.

For real outline fonts, ask designers to approve one or more acceptable decompositions. Multiple acceptable trajectories should not be forced into one supposed ground truth. Compare both geometry and authoring decisions.

### Proposed evaluation dimensions

| Dimension | Proposed evidence | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Geometric placement | Symmetric distance measures and maximum local error | Mean error can hide a broken terminal |
| Shape reconstruction | Area overlap plus boundary-distance error under a declared footprint | Tests how much visual information was preserved |
| Topology | Components, counters, cycles, junction decisions | A tiny geometric error can change the letter |
| Stroke semantics | Lifts, direction, start point, continuity, retracing | Determines actual pen behavior |
| Profile quality | Width error, overlap handling, discontinuity and seam checks | Prevents false weight or pressure effects |
| Font completeness | Glyph inventory, Unicode, alternates, ligatures, metrics | Prevents a drawing demo from being mistaken for a font |
| Layout | Source-shaped and derivative-shaped glyph runs | Tests contextual behavior and spacing |
| Numerical stability | Resolution, threshold, sampling, and parameter perturbations | Identifies brittle candidates |
| Editability | Curve count, nodes, and correction effort | Determines whether human review scales |
| Review yield | Accepted glyphs/families and minutes of correction | Gives a practical cost measure for bulk work |

Do not report only Chamfer distance, pixel error, or a beautiful sample word. Those can conceal wrong graph connectivity, lost alternates, or many unnecessary pen lifts.

### Separate inference error from output flattening

If a future consumer plots at cap height \(H\) mm and the font cap height is \(h\) units, the uniform scale is \(k=H/h\). A fitting deviation of \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{fu}}\) units then corresponds to \(k\epsilon_{\mathrm{fu}}\) mm.

For example, at an 8 mm cap height and a 700-unit cap height, a 0.02 mm **illustrative** fitting target corresponds to 1.75 font units. This is a numerical example, not a recommended tolerance for every plotter or an achieved result.

Raster extraction, skeleton approximation, Bézier fitting, and final physical flattening have separate error contributions. Budget and measure them separately. A valid curve-flattening tolerance cannot repair an incorrect inferred stroke.

## 13. Reuse terms and practical compatibility

Converting an OFL font into another font format is covered by the license's concept of a modified version. Retain the required copyright and license notices, keep the derivative under OFL, and respect any Reserved Font Names. A reusable glyph library differs from an ordinary document rendered with a font. Do not relicense converted glyph data under OpenPlotFont's MIT code license. [Official OFL text](https://openfontlicense.org/open-font-license-official-text/)

Inspect each source family's actual license and revision. Google Fonts catalog membership and a converter's license are not substitutes for that inspection. Pinyon provides a concrete example: a legacy source notice reserves “Pinyon Script”, while the inspected current Google Fonts notice has a 2024 copyright and no explicit Reserved Font Name declaration. The generic OFL clauses about reserved names do not reserve a name by themselves. Pin the notice accompanying the exact source rather than transferring assumptions between revisions. [Legacy Pinyon source](https://github.com/librefonts/pinyonscript), [current Google Fonts notice](https://github.com/google/fonts/blob/main/ofl/pinyonscript/OFL.txt), [OFL FAQ](https://openfontlicense.org/ofl-faq/)

Tool code, model weights, benchmark images, and output font designs can have different terms. FloMat's repository identifies MIT; StrokeStyles identifies GPL-3.0. Incorporating research code into this repository needs a concrete dependency and license review. This study does not choose a license or propose copying either implementation. [FloMat](https://github.com/FlorisSteenkamp/MAT), [StrokeStyles](https://github.com/autograff-devel/strokestyles)

Compatibility still to establish includes current Python and native-library builds, Apple Silicon behavior, Glyphs 4 script/plugin behavior, profile round trips, and font-specific acceptance. The research publications do not certify these integration properties.

## 14. Decision and proposed sequence

**Start with a small, reviewed corpus, not the entire handwriting category.** A carefully selected baseline family, a contextual family, and a high-contrast formal script reveal different failures. Quality should be measured per glyph and per family.

My proposed sequence is:

1. Inventory sources and check whether skeletons or brush data survive upstream.
2. Evaluate StrokeStyles for font decomposition and width profiles.
3. Compare a simple raster baseline with FloMat for geometric fidelity.
4. Evaluate the 2025 traversal method on monoline crossings and loops.
5. Review ambiguous candidates in Glyphs, preserving reference outlines.
6. Measure correction effort before deciding whether a large library is economical.
7. Design a versioned geometric-profile extension only after the candidate data reveals its real needs.
8. Consider the 2026 semantic/differentiable methods when their additional complexity addresses measured failures.

There is no supported conversion-rate or runtime estimate yet. Build a reliable adaptation workflow around evidence and review, then determine how much can be automated.

For an immediate constant-width pen, a reviewed centerline adaptation can preserve much of a script's character even though its original contrast is absent. For future expressive rendering, retaining width evidence, uncertainty, and an explicit footprint model is more durable than inventing physical pressure values from the outline.

## 15. Reading priorities and evidence limits

Read **StrokeStyles** first for the font-specific inverse problem, **Single-Line Drawing Vectorization** for traversal, **FloMat** for a concrete curved-boundary engine, and **InkML** for independent channel semantics. The pruning papers and 2026 vectorization work then deepen the geometric and optimization choices.

This study draws on primary papers, author-maintained repositories, and official documentation. Publication dates were checked against the source pages rather than search-engine crawl labels. Recommendations and the proposed architecture are a synthesis, not claims copied from any one paper.

No candidate algorithm was installed or run. No source family received a visual conversion-quality audit. There is no exhaustive proof that a newer or specialized private tool does not exist, and no common benchmark here that justifies an absolute “best algorithm” ranking. The defensible conclusion is that several strong components exist, while dependable bulk script-font adaptation still needs measured evaluation and a review workflow.
